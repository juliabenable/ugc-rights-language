UGC Rights — how to bill it

A new brand's deal pays creators and adds $50–100/creator for rights to the videos, on top of the monthly subscription. This doc answers the open question from the Aug 7 call — how the money should actually flow — and maps how every comparable platform charges brands for creator money and rights money. Companion to the market study, which covers what rights cost; this one covers how they're billed.

The one-line answer

One monthly invoice, three separate lines: subscription, creator payments, content rights — the last two passed through at face value with $0 added, billed only for creators who confirmed and delivered. The industry hides its take inside bundled prices or stacks 8–30% on top; Benable's contracts already promise "no extra charges on our end," and a line-itemed invoice is what makes that promise visible.

Corollary: separate lines aren't just a sales flex — New York taxes the SaaS line and excludes advertising services, and a Jan 2026 NY ruling (Beeline.com) taxed an entire bundled SaaS-plus-services price because it wasn't separable. The invoice that proves the pass-through is the same invoice that survives a tax audit.

02 —

The five decisions

Everything "how should we bill this" actually contains, decided.

№ 1 · structure

Line items on the monthly invoice

Subscription + creator pass-through + rights pass-through, one Stripe invoice. Don't fold rights into the subscription price (kills comparability, muddies tax) and don't send a second bill (doubles the brand's AP work).

Why: contract language already promises it; NY tax law rewards it; the invoice becomes the proof of "nothing added."
№ 2 · timing

Arrears now, book-ahead later

Now: bill actuals on the next monthly invoice — smallest ops, matches the drafted brand language. Later: when a campaign's creator budget gets real (or a brand has no payment history), collect the budget at campaign kickoff — that's the industry's escrow norm (#paid's deposit, Insense's wallet, JoinBrands' charge-on-accept).

Why: arrears trades a tiny float for zero refund-handling — you never bill for a creator who declined.
№ 3 · trigger

Confirmed + delivered, only

A rights fee is owed the moment the creator has said yes and the file is in hand — that's also the moment the creator gets paid. No charge for declines, no-shows, or posts the brand didn't buy.

Why: "you're only ever billed for the ones we secure" is already in the brand emails — billing mechanics must keep the copy honest.
№ 4 · consent

Bundled deals move consent into the invite

The manual test is post-hoc (brand picks posts after they're live, we ask creators). This deal bundles rights into the campaign — so disclosure belongs in the campaign invite: "this campaign includes [Brand] buying 12-month usage rights — an extra $75 on top of your payment." Accepting the campaign = accepting the rights sale. Fee still pays on delivery.

Why: the rights-included model is how Insense / JoinBrands / minisocial brief; springing it post-hoc on a paid campaign invites declines the brand already paid to avoid.
№ 5 · rail

PayPal Payouts — covers Venmo too

The open Venmo-vs-PayPal question has a one-rail answer: PayPal's Payouts product sends to PayPal and Venmo recipients (recipient_wallet: VENMO, US-only). Creator picks; one integration, one ledger. This is literally Aspire's setup.

Why: PayPal is the default rail for this entire platform class (Aspire, GRIN, JoinBrands, Insense, Billo).
flow a · the manual test (live now)

Post-hoc — brand picks after posts are live

  1. Campaign runs, posts go live
  2. Brand replies with links to posts they want
  3. Creator gets the SMS ask → YES = the license
  4. File lands → creator paid same day (Venmo/PayPal)
  5. $75 × n confirmed → next monthly invoice
flow b · this deal (rights bundled)

Bundled — rights disclosed in the invite

  1. Campaign invite states it: payment + $75 rights fee, 12-month license, raw file due
  2. Creator accepts campaign = consents to the rights sale
  3. Content delivered + approved, raw file in
  4. Creator paid: campaign fee + rights fee, same payout
  5. Both amounts → next monthly invoice, separate lines

04 —

Three patterns worth stealing

i.

Charge on confirmation, release on approval

Universal, from Upwork's escrow to JoinBrands' charge-on-accept to TRIBE's pay-per-approved-submission. Nobody bills for a creator who declined. Benable's "only confirmed posts are billed" already matches the market's best behavior — keep it as the invariant no billing change may break.

ii.

A separate rights line is the premium move

Downmarket bundles rights invisibly; only the premium tier (TRIBE, #paid) bills rights as their own purchase. Benable runs the premium mechanic at a pass-through price — the exact unclaimed wedge the market study named.

Steal: TRIBE charges at the approval click. When the portal productizes rights, "confirm creator" = the charge moment.

iii.

Aspire is the structural twin — then the fork

Creator gets 100%, fees sit brand-side, PayPal Payouts rail, PayPal absorbs tax forms. Copy it wholesale. The later monetization fork: a take-rate on pass-through (industry 8–30%) vs a rights-included premium tier (Social Cat's model).

Signal: Chiara said she'd pay for a pricier Benable plan with rights included — one real data point for the tier path over the take-rate path.

05 —

Ops watch-outs

Published facts for finance to confirm — not legal or tax advice. The two starred items are where a CPA makes the call.

Tax forms: near-zero duty, collect W-9s anyway

Current IRS instructions put the 1099-NEC threshold at $2,000/creator/year for payments made in 2026 (up from $600, per the 2025 tax bill), and the 1099-K TPSO threshold reverted to >$20k AND >200 transactions. Payments settled through a third-party network (PayPal) are the network's to report, not the payer's.

Still: the Upwork / GRIN / Fiverr norm is W-9 before the first dollar moves — it costs nothing, covers the 24% backup-withholding rule, and repeat paid creators can cross $2k.

irs.gov/instructions/i1099mec · IRS FS-2025-08 · paypal.com 1099-K help

★ CPA call: GRIN self-issues 1099-NECs; Aspire issues nothing and points at PayPal's 1099-K. Same rail, opposite positions — both published. Pick one deliberately.

Sales tax: separate lines are load-bearing

NY taxes SaaS including remote access (TB-ST-128) but excludes advertising / representative services (20 NYCRR 527.3). In Beeline.com (NY App. Div., Jan 15 2026), a SaaS-plus-services bundle was taxed in full because the software was the "true object" and the parts weren't separately stated.

Stripe Tax applies a tax code per line item — SaaS line taxable, creator/rights lines mapped to service codes. The invoice structure in § 01 is what makes that mapping possible.

tax.ny.gov TB-ST-128 · 20 NYCRR 527.3 · docs.stripe.com/tax

★ CPA call: confirm the pass-through lines' code mapping per state (brand billing addresses: NY precedent already open from the Pair deal).

Keep pass-through out of revenue

With no markup and no pricing discretion, Benable is an agent under ASC 606 — the pass-through is cash-in / liability / cash-out, and only the subscription is revenue. PwC's guidance: gross-vs-net "is not a choice," it follows control. Practically: the $450 rights line never touches MRR, and dashboards that mix it in will overstate the business.

06 —

One language flag before the contract ships

"Full ownership" isn't what we decided to sell

The deal memo says the brand pays for "full ownership rights." The Aug 7 decision was deliberately narrower: raw files delivered outright + a 12-month worldwide license (owned channels + paid ads), renewable through Benable — with "perpetual" banned from all copy. The market study prices true perpetuity at 2–5× a timed license; $50–100 for actual ownership would undersell creators and reopen the one complaint that can poison the creator side ("my face in their ads forever, for $75").

Keep the 12-month clock unless this brand's contract explicitly promised assignment. The renewal is also the future revenue moment — Amazon runs 90-day licenses with a preset extension fee for exactly this reason.
